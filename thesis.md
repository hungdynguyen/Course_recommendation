\subsection{The significance of the topic}
Recent reports from many international organizations show that online education has expanded to an unprecedented scale. Based on UNESCO data (2023) \cite{unesco2023gem}, over 220 million learners participated in MOOCs globally by 2021. At the same time, leading platforms have attracted tens of millions of users. For example, Coursera reported approximately 142 million registered learners by the end of 2023 \cite{coursera2023financial}. These numbers clearly illustrate the massive scale of online education, confirming that digital learning has become a mainstream educational pathway rather than a supplementary one. This rapid growth is fundamentally driven by the accelerating pace of skill obsolescence in the modern labor market. According to the World Economic Forum \cite{wef2023}, it is estimated that 44\% of workers' core skills will be disrupted by 2027 due to the rapid integration of emerging technologies. This growth is further propelled by the relentless emergence of novel professional competencies. According to the latest industry data from LinkedIn’s Economic Graph \cite{linkedin2025}, the skill sets required for global occupations have evolved significantly, with a projected change of 68\% by 2030—an acceleration driven largely by the integration of generative AI into the workforce. As a result, the digital learning landscape is constantly changing with new topics, requiring learners to master skills that did not even exist in traditional academic programs just a few years ago.

However, the massive expansion of course catalogs has led to serious information overload. Research has shown that learners in MOOC environments struggle to identify suitable courses as the number of available options increases dramatically. 
One direct consequence is low engagement. Studies report that only 7–13 \% of enrolled learners actually complete MOOC courses \cite{mooc_completion_gamification}, indicating that many users either choose unsuitable courses or lose interest after enrollment. Traditional recommendation approaches — mainly collaborative filtering or simple content matching — rely heavily on past interactions and therefore struggle when user preferences are unclear or when courses are newly introduced. Consequently, recommendation quality remains limited and learner motivation decreases.

To overcome these challenges, this research leverages the potential of contrastive learning and graph-based reasoning. Specifically, contrastive learning is employed to refine representation quality by filtering inherent noise and capturing semantic similarities between courses, even those spanning diverse subject areas. This allows models to achieve better performance in cold-start scenarios where interaction data is minimal. On the other hand, graph-based reasoning enhances the system by modeling semantic connections between learners, courses, skills rather than depending only on past interactions. This approach enables the system to infer relationships and support recommendations that actually consider the prerequisite relationship between courses. Consequently, these advanced methods not only improve recommendation accuracy but also ensure explainability, as recommended items can be traced through meaningful relational paths.

\subsection{Overview of previous studies}

Recommender systems have been widely studied to address the problem of information overload in online platforms. Over the past two decades, the field has evolved through several major paradigms, ranging from rule-based approaches to modern deep learning and graph-based recommendation models.

Early recommender systems were primarily based on rule-based or knowledge-based approaches. These systems relied on manually designed rules and domain knowledge to match user profiles with suitable items. Although such systems were interpretable and easy to implement, they lacked adaptability and scalability when dealing with large and dynamic datasets.

Content-based filtering (CBF) was one of the earliest data-driven approaches. In this paradigm, recommendations are generated by comparing the features of items that a user has previously interacted with and suggesting similar items \cite{lops2011content}. The system constructs a user profile based on item attributes such as keywords, topics, or descriptions. However, content-based approaches often suffer from overspecialization, as they tend to recommend items that are very similar to those previously consumed.

Collaborative filtering (CF) later became the dominant paradigm in recommender systems. CF methods leverage the behavior of similar users to predict a user's preference for unseen items. Traditional neighborhood-based approaches compute similarities between users or items, while model-based approaches such as matrix factorization learn latent representations of users and items from interaction data \cite{koren2009matrix}. Matrix factorization techniques gained widespread adoption due to their effectiveness in large-scale recommendation tasks such as the Netflix Prize competition.

Despite their success, traditional CF methods suffer from several limitations, including data sparsity and cold-start problems. To overcome these issues, hybrid recommender systems were proposed to combine collaborative filtering with content-based signals \cite{burke2002hybrid}. Hybrid models integrate multiple information sources to improve recommendation accuracy and coverage.

With the rapid growth of deep learning, neural recommender systems have been proposed to model complex user-item interactions. These approaches utilize neural networks to learn high-dimensional representations of users and items from implicit feedback and contextual information. Recent research has shown that deep learning models can capture nonlinear relationships in recommendation data and outperform traditional models in many scenarios.

More recently, graph-based recommendation methods have emerged as a powerful paradigm. These methods model recommender systems as graphs consisting of users, items, and additional entities such as skills, topics, or knowledge concepts. Knowledge graph-based recommender systems incorporate structured relationships between entities to enhance recommendation quality \cite{wang2019kgat}. By propagating information through graph structures, these models can capture higher-order relationships between users and items.

In addition, knowledge graph reasoning methods have been proposed to improve interpretability and performance. For example, path-based reasoning over knowledge graphs can explain the rationale behind recommendations and model semantic relationships between entities \cite{wang2019kprn}. These approaches demonstrate the growing importance of structured knowledge in modern recommender systems.

Overall, the evolution of recommender systems reflects a shift from rule-based heuristics toward data-driven and knowledge-aware models. While recent graph-based approaches have shown promising results, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved.

\subsection{Research gaps}

Despite the significant progress in recommender system research, several important limitations remain. These limitations motivate the development of more advanced recommendation frameworks, particularly in domains such as education and career planning.

First, existing recommender systems often suffer from semantic limitations when dealing with domain-specific knowledge. Many modern recommender systems rely on general-purpose embedding models to represent users and items. However, these embeddings may fail to capture the precise meaning of domain-specific concepts such as technical skills, job roles, or specialized topics. As a result, semantically related concepts in a particular domain may not be correctly represented in the embedding space. This issue becomes especially critical in education and career recommendation scenarios where subtle semantic differences between skills and topics can significantly impact recommendation relevance.

Second, traditional recommender systems typically treat items as independent entities, focusing primarily on historical user-item interactions or surface-level content similarity. However, in educational and career development contexts, courses and skills are inherently interlinked through complex prerequisite structures and hierarchical dependencies. While recent Knowledge Graph-based (KG) recommender systems have attempted to incorporate relational data \cite{wang2019kgat}, many of these approaches still prioritize maximizing prediction accuracy through latent embeddings—essentially treating the graph as a "black-box" feature enhancer. Consequently, these models lack an explicit reasoning mechanism to bridge the gap between a user's current capabilities and their target career goals. There is a significant research gap in developing frameworks capable of recursive logical traversal across skill-course dependencies. Such a mechanism is essential to ensure that the recommended sequence is not only semantically relevant to the target job but also pedagogically accessible, ensuring that foundational skills are acquired before advanced content is introduced.

Third, recommender systems frequently suffer from the lack of labeled training data. Collaborative filtering methods rely heavily on historical interaction data such as ratings, clicks, or enrollments. However, in many domains, especially emerging educational platforms or specialized learning environments, labeled interaction data can be sparse or unavailable. This data sparsity problem leads to cold-start issues for both new users and new items. Although knowledge graph reasoning methods like KPRN \cite{wang2019kprn} have been proposed to mitigate sparsity, they still predominantly depend on supervised signals to learn path preferences. There is a pressing need for a framework that capable of capturing the inherent relationships between skills and courses without the necessity for large-scale, labeled interaction histories.

In summary, three major research gaps remain in existing recommender systems: (1) the inability of general embedding models to capture domain-specific semantics, (2) the lack of mechanisms to model structural dependencies between knowledge units, and (3) the scarcity of labeled interaction data for training recommendation models. Addressing these challenges is essential for building more accurate and explainable recommendation systems in knowledge-intensive domains such as education and career guidance.

\subsection{Objectives and Research Questions}

\subsubsection{Objectives}
The main objective of this research is to build a complete course recommendation system that helps individuals advance in their professional career paths. To achieve this, the study focuses on the following specific goals:
\begin{itemize}
\item To develop a domain-adaptive embedding model for specialized skills and qualifications. This model will be fine-tuned on domain-specific document to capture nuanced skill semantics that general-purpose language models often miss.
\item To construct a knowledge graph of courses and skills that encodes prerequisite and dependency relationships. By representing courses as nodes linked by prerequisite edges, the system can generate recommendations that respect the inherent learning sequence of educational content.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Research Questions}
The research questions addressed in this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How can domain-adaptive contrastive learning improve the representation of specific skills and thus the relevance of course recommendations?
\item In what ways can knowledge-graph reasoning with prerequisite constraints enhance the logical ordering and coherence of recommended course sequences?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Large Language Models}

\subsubsection{Definition and Conceptual Framework}
Large Language Models (LLMs) represent an advanced class of artificial intelligence systems designed to process, interpret, and generate human-like text by learning from massive datasets. Unlike traditional Natural Language Processing (NLP) models that were often task-specific, LLMs are characterized by their immense scale—often containing billions of parameters—and their training on diverse, large-scale corpora \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}. At their technical core, these models are deep neural networks that function as probabilistic engines, calculating the likelihood of a subsequent token in a sequence based on the preceding context. A defining trait of modern LLMs is the emergence of "emergent abilities," such as complex reasoning and in-context learning, which typically manifest only when the model reaches a certain threshold of computational scale and data volume \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}.

\subsubsection{Historical Evolution and the Transformer Breakthrough}
The trajectory of language modeling has transitioned from early statistical approaches to neural-based architectures. However, a fundamental shift occurred with the introduction of the Transformer architecture by Vaswani et al. (2017). Prior to this, the field relied heavily on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, which processed data sequentially. This sequential nature posed a significant bottleneck for parallelization and limited the model's ability to capture dependencies between distant words in a sentence \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

The Transformer eliminated recurrence entirely, opting instead for a mechanism based solely on attention. This innovation allowed for significantly higher parallelization during training and established the foundation for the current generation of models. Following this breakthrough, the field moved into the era of Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs), such as T5 and early GPT versions, which utilized self-supervised learning on large datasets to create general-purpose representations. This evolution eventually led to the contemporary LLM era, where models like GPT-4 and PaLM-2 demonstrate near-human performance across a wide spectrum of cognitive tasks \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Foundations of the Attention Mechanism}
The operational efficiency of LLMs is primarily derived from the \textit{Scaled Dot-Product Attention} mechanism. This mathematical framework allows the model to assign varying degrees of importance to different parts of the input sequence regardless of their distance \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

The process begins by mapping each input token into three distinct vectors: a Query ($Q$), a Key ($K$), and a Value ($V$). For a set of queries packed into a matrix, the attention output is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}

In this formulation, $d_k$ represents the dimension of the keys. The dot product of $Q$ and $K$ determines the compatibility (or alignment) between tokens. The result is divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$ as a scaling factor to prevent the gradients from becoming extremely small during training, ensuring numerical stability. The \text{softmax} function then normalizes these scores into weights that sum to one, which are finally used to produce a weighted sum of the values ($V$).

To enhance the model’s ability to focus on different positions and representation subspaces simultaneously, LLMs employ \textit{Multi-Head Attention}. This involves performing the attention function in parallel across $h$ different projections:

\begin{equation}
\text{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)W^O
\end{equation}

where each $\text{head}_i$ is an independent attention operation. This architectural design allows the model to integrate information from multiple contextual perspectives, which is crucial for understanding complex linguistic structures \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

\subsubsection{Adaptation Regimes and Alignment}
The development of a functional LLM typically involves multiple stages. The primary stage is \textbf{Pre-training}, where the model learns fundamental language patterns through self-supervised objectives like Causal Language Modeling (predicting the next token) or Masked Language Modeling \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}. 

However, pre-trained models may not always follow specific user instructions or may generate harmful content. To rectify this, models undergo \textbf{Fine-tuning} and \textbf{Alignment}. Techniques such as Instruction Tuning use human-curated prompt-response pairs to teach the model how to respond to queries. Furthermore, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is employed to align model outputs with human values (helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness) by training a reward model to rank generated responses \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}.

\subsubsection{Diverse Applications of LLMs}
The versatility of LLMs has enabled their integration into numerous professional and creative domains:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{General Purpose and Knowledge Management:} LLMs serve as advanced assistants for summarization, translation, and information retrieval, filtering vast amounts of data to provide concise insights \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}.
    \item \textbf{Software Development:} Specialized models like AlphaCode and Codex facilitate automated code generation, debugging, and multi-turn program synthesis, significantly augmenting developer productivity.
    \item \textbf{Professional Domains (Medicine, Law, Finance):} In healthcare, LLMs assist in clinical decision support and medical research synthesis. In the legal sector, they are used for thematic analysis of legal documents and case law explanation. Financial models like BloombergGPT utilize industry-specific datasets for sentiment analysis and algorithmic trading support \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}.
    \item \textbf{Robotics and Embodied AI:} LLMs act as the "high-level brain" for autonomous agents, translating natural language instructions into actionable plans for robot manipulation and navigation \cite{naveed2025comprehensive}.
\end{itemize}

By combining the structural efficiency of the Transformer with massive scaling and sophisticated alignment techniques, LLMs have evolved from simple text predictors into powerful reasoning engines capable of reshaping modern technology.

\subsection{Embedding Models}

\subsubsection{Definitions and History}

Embedding models aim to represent discrete data (e.g., words, sentences, or entities) as continuous vectors in a dense, low-dimensional space, such that semantic similarity is reflected by geometric proximity \cite{mikolov2013efficient, pennington2014glove}. 

Before neural embedding models, text was commonly represented using sparse methods such as one-hot encoding or bag-of-words. \textbf{Early Distributed Representations (Pre-2013)} suffer from high dimensionality and an inability to capture semantic similarity. Early efforts to learn distributed representations include neural probabilistic language models \cite{bengio2003neural}, which introduced the idea of mapping words into continuous vector spaces. However, these methods were computationally expensive and limited in scalability.

A major breakthrough came with the introduction of \textbf{Static Word Embeddings (2013--2016)} such as Word2Vec \cite{mikolov2013efficient}, which proposed efficient training objectives such as Skip-gram and Continuous Bag-of-Words (CBOW) to learn word embeddings from large corpora. These models demonstrated that semantic relationships could be captured through vector arithmetic. Shortly after, GloVe \cite{pennington2014glove} improved upon this by incorporating global word co-occurrence statistics, providing more stable and interpretable embeddings. Despite their effectiveness, these embeddings are static, meaning each word has a single representation regardless of context.

The next major advancement addressed the limitations of static embeddings by introducing \textbf{Contextualized Embeddings Models (2018--2019)}. ELMo \cite{peters2018deep} leveraged deep bidirectional language models to generate embeddings conditioned on the entire sentence. This idea was further advanced by BERT \cite{devlin2019bert}, which employed the Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention} and self-supervised objectives such as masked language modeling. These models produce dynamic embeddings that vary depending on the surrounding context, significantly improving performance across a wide range of NLP tasks.

While contextual models provide powerful token-level representations, they are not inherently optimized for sentence-level similarity tasks. \textbf{Sentence-BERT} \cite{reimers2019sentence} addressed this limitation by introducing a siamese network structure with contrastive learning objectives, enabling efficient computation of semantically meaningful sentence embeddings. Concurrently, dense retrieval models such as DPR \cite{karpukhin2020dense} adopted dual-encoder architectures to learn embeddings for queries and documents, facilitating scalable semantic search through vector similarity.

Recent advances have emphasized \textbf{contrastive learning (2020--Present)} as a central paradigm for embedding training. Frameworks such as SimCLR \cite{chen2020simple} demonstrated the effectiveness of learning representations by maximizing agreement between positive pairs while minimizing similarity to negative samples. This approach has been widely adopted in both vision and language domains, including models such as CLIP \cite{radford2021learning}, which aligns text and image embeddings in a shared space. 

In the context of language, modern embedding models increasingly rely on large-scale pretraining combined with contrastive fine-tuning to produce high-quality representations for tasks such as semantic matching, clustering, and deduplication. Recent works have also explored instruction-tuned and domain-specific embeddings, enabling improved performance in specialized applications such as skill representation and job matching.

\subsubsection{Model Architectures}

Modern embedding models are predominantly based on the Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}, which enables efficient modeling of contextual dependencies through self-attention mechanisms. Transformer-based encoders, such as BERT, generate contextualized token representations that can be aggregated (e.g., via pooling) to produce sentence-level embeddings.

Sentence embedding models typically adopt encoder-only architectures for efficiency in retrieval scenarios. Variants such as Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sentence} employ siamese networks, where two or more input sequences are encoded independently using shared parameters, and their embeddings are compared using similarity metrics such as cosine similarity.

Recent advances have introduced specialized architectures and training strategies for embedding learning, including dual-encoder frameworks for large-scale retrieval systems, where query and document embeddings are computed independently to enable efficient nearest neighbor search \cite{karpukhin2020dense}.

\subsubsection{Training Regimes}

The training of embedding models has evolved from unsupervised learning to more sophisticated regimes incorporating supervised and self-supervised signals. Early models such as Word2Vec and GloVe rely on unsupervised learning from raw text corpora \cite{mikolov2013efficient, pennington2014glove}.

Contextual models like BERT are trained using self-supervised objectives on large-scale corpora, enabling them to learn rich linguistic representations without manual annotation \cite{devlin2019bert}. However, for tasks requiring fine-grained semantic alignment, additional fine-tuning is often necessary.

Recent approaches emphasize contrastive learning frameworks, where models are trained on pairs or triplets of data to explicitly encode similarity relationships. This paradigm has been shown to significantly improve performance in semantic matching, clustering, and retrieval tasks \cite{chen2020simple, karpukhin2020dense}. Furthermore, domain-specific embedding models can be obtained by fine-tuning on task-specific datasets, allowing the embedding space to better capture domain-relevant semantics, such as skills or job-related concepts.

Overall, the combination of large-scale pretraining and task-specific contrastive fine-tuning has emerged as a dominant paradigm for learning high-quality embeddings suitable for downstream applications.

\subsubsection{Applications}

 Semantic matching is one of the most prominent applications of embedding models, where the goal is to measure the similarity between two pieces of text based on meaning rather than surface form. Traditional lexical matching approaches, such as keyword-based retrieval, often fail to capture paraphrases or semantic equivalence. Embedding models address this limitation by representing semantically similar texts as nearby vectors in the embedding space.

Sentence-level embedding models, such as Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sentence}, have been specifically designed to support efficient semantic similarity computation using distance metrics such as cosine similarity. These models are widely used in applications such as semantic search, question answering, and recommendation systems. In large-scale retrieval settings, dual-encoder architectures \cite{karpukhin2020dense} enable fast nearest-neighbor search, making them suitable for real-time matching tasks involving large corpora.

Embedding models also play a crucial role in clustering tasks, where the objective is to group semantically similar items without explicit supervision. By transforming textual data into dense vector representations, embeddings make it possible to apply standard clustering algorithms such as k-means or hierarchical clustering in a meaningful semantic space.

In this context, the quality of embeddings directly impacts the coherence and separability of clusters. Contextual and contrastively trained embeddings have been shown to significantly improve clustering performance by better capturing semantic relationships \cite{chen2020simple}. Applications of clustering with embeddings include topic discovery, taxonomy construction, and organization of large-scale text collections, such as grouping similar skills or job descriptions into coherent categories.

Deduplication is another critical application of embedding models, particularly in domains with noisy or redundant data. The goal is to identify and remove duplicate or near-duplicate entries that may differ in wording but share the same semantic meaning.

Embedding-based deduplication relies on computing similarity scores between items and identifying pairs that exceed a predefined threshold. Unlike exact string matching, this approach is robust to variations in phrasing, spelling, and formatting. Recent embedding models trained with contrastive objectives are especially effective in this setting, as they explicitly enforce similarity between semantically equivalent inputs while separating unrelated ones.

This capability is particularly valuable in structured domains such as skill normalization, where the same skill may be expressed in multiple ways. Embedding-based deduplication enables the consolidation of such variations into canonical representations, improving data quality and downstream retrieval performance.

Overall, embedding models provide a unified framework for semantic matching, clustering, and deduplication by encoding meaning into a continuous vector space. Their effectiveness in these tasks has made them a cornerstone of modern retrieval and representation systems, particularly when combined with contrastive learning techniques that enhance the alignment of the embedding space with semantic similarity objectives.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph}

\subsubsection{Definition}

A Knowledge Graph (KG) is a structured representation of real-world entities and their relationships, typically modeled as a graph where nodes represent entities and edges represent relations between them. Formally, a KG is often defined as a set of triples $(h, r, t)$, where $h$ (head entity) and $t$ (tail entity) are connected by a relation $r$ \cite{nickel2015review}.

Knowledge Graphs are designed to integrate heterogeneous information into a unified representation, enabling machines to perform reasoning, semantic search, and knowledge discovery. Unlike traditional relational databases, KGs emphasize semantic relationships and flexible schema design, making them particularly suitable for complex, interconnected data \cite{hogan2021knowledge}.

In recent years, Knowledge Graph Embedding (KGE) methods have been introduced to map entities and relations into continuous vector spaces while preserving the structural information of the graph. These embeddings facilitate downstream tasks such as link prediction, entity classification, and recommendation \cite{wang2017knowledge}. Popular approaches include translational distance models such as TransE, which interpret relations as translations in embedding space \cite{bordes2013transe}, as well as more advanced models like RotatE \cite{sun2019rotate}.

\subsubsection{History}

The concept of representing knowledge as graphs has its roots in early semantic networks and symbolic AI research in the 1970s and 1980s. However, modern Knowledge Graphs gained prominence with the rise of large-scale web data and the need for structured knowledge representation.

A major milestone was the introduction of large, publicly available Knowledge Graphs such as DBpedia \cite{auer2007dbpedia}, which extracted structured information from Wikipedia, and Freebase \cite{bollacker2008freebase}, a collaboratively curated knowledge base. These projects demonstrated the feasibility of building large-scale KGs and enabled widespread research in the field.

The term “Knowledge Graph” became widely popular after Google introduced its Knowledge Graph in 2012 to enhance search engine capabilities by providing semantic search results and entity-based answers. This marked a shift from keyword-based retrieval to entity-centric information systems.

Subsequently, research focus shifted toward representation learning on Knowledge Graphs. The introduction of TransE \cite{bordes2013transe} marked a turning point by enabling scalable embedding of large graphs. This was followed by a surge of embedding models, including TransH, TransR, and RotatE \cite{sun2019rotate}, which improved the ability to model complex relations.

More recently, Knowledge Graph research has expanded into integration with deep learning and large language models, as well as applications in recommendation systems, question answering, and reasoning. Comprehensive surveys such as \cite{nickel2015review, wang2017knowledge, hogan2021knowledge} summarize the evolution of the field and highlight ongoing challenges such as scalability, incompleteness, and dynamic updates.

\subsubsection{Applications}

Knowledge Graphs (KGs) have been widely adopted across a variety of domains due to their ability to represent structured, semantic relationships between entities. Their applications span multiple tasks in artificial intelligence, data mining, and information retrieval.

One of the most prominent applications of KGs is in \textbf{search and information retrieval}. By leveraging entity-centric representations, KGs enable semantic search that goes beyond keyword matching, allowing systems to understand user intent and return more relevant results. This paradigm has been successfully deployed in industrial systems such as web search engines \cite{hogan2021knowledge}.

KGs also play a critical role in \textbf{question answering (QA)} systems. By structuring knowledge into triples, KGs allow QA systems to perform multi-hop reasoning over entities and relations to derive answers. Approaches range from symbolic traversal-based reasoning to embedding-based methods that rank candidate answers \cite{nickel2015review}.

Another important application is \textbf{recommendation systems}. Knowledge Graph-based recommendation methods enhance traditional collaborative filtering by incorporating rich semantic information about users, items, and their relationships. Techniques include path-based reasoning, meta-path exploration, and embedding-based approaches that learn latent representations of entities \cite{wang2019kgat}. These methods improve recommendation accuracy, interpretability, and the ability to handle data sparsity.

In \textbf{natural language processing (NLP)}, KGs are used to enhance language understanding by providing structured background knowledge. They are commonly integrated into tasks such as named entity recognition, entity linking, and text generation, where external knowledge improves contextual understanding \cite{ji2021survey}.

KGs are also widely applied in \textbf{knowledge graph completion} and \textbf{link prediction}, where the goal is to infer missing relationships between entities. Embedding models such as TransE and RotatE learn continuous representations that capture graph structure and enable prediction of unseen triples \cite{bordes2013transe, sun2019rotate}.

In addition, KGs support \textbf{reasoning and inference}, including logical reasoning and rule-based traversal over graph structures. These capabilities are particularly useful in domains such as healthcare, finance, and recommendation systems, where explainability and structured decision-making are important \cite{hogan2021knowledge}.

Finally, KGs are increasingly integrated with \textbf{deep learning and large language models}, enabling hybrid systems that combine symbolic reasoning with neural representations. This integration allows models to leverage both structured knowledge and unstructured text, improving performance across a wide range of tasks \cite{ji2021survey}.

Overall, the versatility of Knowledge Graphs makes them a foundational component in modern intelligent systems, supporting a wide spectrum of applications that require structured knowledge representation and reasoning.

\subsection{Training Dataset for Skill Representation Learning}

\subsubsection{Data Description}
To provide a consistent and high-quality reference space, skills are anchored to the ESCO (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations) taxonomy. ESCO is a widely recognized and authoritative multilingual classification system developed by the European Commission, offering a comprehensive and structured vocabulary of skills, occupations, and their relationships across the European labour market and education systems \cite{panzaru2025esco}. Its large-scale coverage, multilingual support, and systematic structure make it a reliable foundation for representing canonical skill concepts.

The dataset is structured as a triplet collection, where each record consists of three elements: a query (natural language expression of a skill), a positive (the corresponding correct ESCO skill description), and a hard negative negative (a semantically close but conceptually distinct skill of the query). This triplet design is grounded in metric learning theory, where models are trained to minimize the distance between query and positive while maximizing distance between query and negative. Each record also carries metadata: the ESCO URI (unique identifier), skill name, skill type (digital, transversal, etc.), alternative labels, source origin (synthetic or human-labeled), and generation timestamp.

An illustrative data example is as follows:
\begin{mbox}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
{
  "query": "extract patterns and insights from large datasets",
  "positive": "Data analysis. The ability to examine and interpret...",
  "negative": "Data management. The ability to organize and store...",
  "metadata": {
      "skill_uri": "http://data.europa.eu/esco/skill/...",
      "skill_name": "data analysis",
      "skill_type": "digital",
      "alt_labels": ["data examination", "data interpretation"],
      "source": "auto_generated",
      "generated_at": "2025-03-23T10:30:00Z"
  }
}
\end{verbatim}
\end{mbox}

The dataset comprises approximately 41,000 auto-generated triplet samples across the ESCO skill taxonomy, split into approximately 33,000 training samples and 8,000 test samples. The training set is reserved for model fine-tuning, while the test set includes both synthetic samples and human-annotated mappings to enable external validation.

\subsubsection{Data Preparation Process}

\begin{figure}[htbp] 
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Figures/training dataset pipeline.png} 
    \caption{Training dataset preparation pipeline} 
\end{figure}


The preparation process begins with systematic synthesis of training supervision from the ESCO taxonomy. For each ESCO skill, the process generates multiple query variations by prompting a large language model to create diverse phrasings that resemble how that skill might be expressed in course descriptions (detailed in Figure \ref{fig:train_data_generate_prompt}). Simultaneously, hard negatives are synthesized by requesting the model to generate semantically related but fundamentally different skill descriptions. 

Prior to training, the synthetic triplets undergo a rigorous quality control process based on distributional analysis. For each sample, the system computes the \textit{semantic margin} $M$, defined as the difference between the query-positive similarity and the query-negative similarity:
\[ M = \text{sim}(Q, P) - \text{sim}(Q, N) \]

This margin serves as the primary metric for assessing supervision quality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Positive Margins ($M > 0$)}: Indicate clear and reliable training signals where the current embedding space correctly prioritizes the positive mapping over the negative one.
    \item \textbf{Moderate Negative Margins ($M < 0$, small absolute value)}: Suggest an insufficiency in the pre-trained model's discriminative power. In these cases, the model fails to capture fine-grained domain nuances, making these samples highly valuable "hard negatives" for further fine-tuning to improve representation boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Extreme Negative Margins ($M \ll 0$)}: Reveal potential \textbf{"False Negatives"}, where the assigned negative sample is conceptually more relevant to the query than the intended positive. Such semantic inversions often stem from synthetic generation errors and introduce contradictory patterns that can degrade embedding quality.
\end{itemize}

To mitigate label noise, the pipeline implements a priority-based audit mechanism. Samples with negative margins are ranked by severity, and a representative subset is manually reviewed. This audit determines whether the hard negatives are genuinely distinct or contextually relevant to the query. Based on the audit findings, a filtering threshold is established to prune low-quality triplets from the training pool. 

To ensure that the test set includes high-confidence, human-validated mappings, a parallel annotation process is executed. Domain annotators review selected course skills paired with embedding-based retrieval results and explicitly select the correct ESCO mapping. These human annotations are stored separately as ground truth and later merged into the test set. Consequently, the resulting dataset provides both the scalability required for deep representation learning and the empirical validity necessary for robust evaluation in specialized labor market and educational contexts.

\subsection{End-to-End Job-to-Course Recommendation Evaluation Dataset}

\subsubsection{Data Description}

The end-to-end evaluation dataset is structured as a collection of \textbf{upskilling scenarios}. Each record represents a complete mapping from a professional requirement to a pedagogical solution, providing a benchmark for the entire recommendation pipeline. 

Each data record consists of the following structured fields:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input - Job Descriptions}: Contains the target role title, a list of required technical and soft skills, and minimum experience/education prerequisites.
    \item \textbf{Input - Candidate Profile}: Represents the learner's current state, including their existing skills, current years of experience, and educational background.
    \item \textbf{Output - Course Suggestions}: A ranked list of courses retrieved from the curated academic catalog.
\end{itemize}

The final dataset comprises approximately \textbf{100 job-candidate scenarios}, spanning multiple industry domains. This structured format allows for the calculation of standard Information Retrieval metrics by comparing the system's ranked output against the human-provided relevance labels.

The pipeline initiates with the processing of authentic job descriptions to derive specific professional requirements. For each job posting, a synthetic candidate profile is generated with a controlled mismatch level, typically ranging from 60\% to 80\% alignment. This threshold is strategically enforced to simulate realistic career transitions and upskilling scenarios, avoiding the uninformative extremes of either perfect matches or complete irrelevance. The resulting candidate profile highlights explicit \textit{Skills Gaps}, identifying the specific technical and soft competencies that the candidate lacks relative to the job's demands, which subsequently serve as the primary query for recommendation. This synthesis step is governed by a structured prompt (detailed in Figure \ref{fig:cv_generation_prompt}).

Simultaneously, the supply-side branch focuses on transforming the raw course catalog into a structured, skill-centric knowledge base. A significant challenge in processing educational catalogs is that course descriptions often exhibit unbounded lengths and contain substantial amounts of non-pedagogical noise, such as instructor biographies, scheduling logistics, and administrative details. To mitigate this noise and ensure data consistency, the pipeline employs the \textbf{Qwen3-14B} large language model (LLM) and the prompt in Figure \ref{fig:skill_extraction_prompt} to perform high-precision skill extraction. By leveraging the model's advanced natural language understanding, the system distills verbose and unstructured curriculum metadata into a concise, classified set of \textit{Course Skills}. This extraction process not only filters out irrelevant information but also provides a standardized semantic representation of each learning module. Consequently, this refined output enables a robust and direct comparison between the core competencies offered by the courses and the previously identified candidate deficiencies.

The final stage of the pipeline is a manual labeling process conducted to create a high-quality evaluation set. Due to the large volume of courses, an initial filtering step was performed using the catalog’s pre-defined categories to remove clearly irrelevant options. The subsequent detailed labeling followed a strict set of rules to ensure accuracy. A course was marked as relevant only if it addressed at least one of the candidate's identified skill gaps. For candidates with lower qualifications, foundational courses were prioritized over advanced ones to ensure a logical learning sequence. To improve efficiency, preference was given to courses that covered the maximum number of missing skills, reducing the total number of required learning modules. In cases where the catalog contained no suitable matches for a specific gap, no recommendation was assigned to maintain the integrity of the ground truth. This careful, rule-based approach resulted in a \textbf{Human-labeled Set} that serves as a reliable benchmark for evaluating the final recommendation system.

\subsection{Domain-Adaptive Contrastive Learning}

We formalize the course recommendation task as a sequential optimization problem within a job-matching context. Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the universal set of professional skills. A user profile is represented by a subset of acquired skills $S_{user} \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, and a target job is defined by a set of required skills $S_{job} \subseteq \mathcal{S}$. The skill gap to be addressed is defined as the set difference: 
\begin{equation}
    \Delta S = S_{job} - S_{user}
\end{equation}

Given a course catalog $\mathcal{C}$, where each course $c \in \mathcal{C}$ is characterized by a set of skills it imparts, $Skills(c) \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, and a set of prerequisite skills, $Pre(c) \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, the objective is to identify an optimal sequence of courses $\mathcal{P} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k\}$ that satisfies two primary conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Coverage:} The union of skills provided by the sequence must cover the skill gap: $\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{k} Skills(c_i) \right) \supseteq \Delta S$.
    \item \textbf{Pedagogical Consistency:} The sequence must respect the directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure of prerequisites. Formally, for any course $c_i \in \mathcal{P}$, all its prerequisite skills must be either already possessed by the user or covered by preceding courses in the sequence: $Pre(c_i) \subseteq \left( S_{user} \cup \bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1} Skills(c_j) \right)$.
\end{enumerate}

This formalization shifts the recommendation goal from traditional top-N ranking to the generation of a \textbf{coherent learning roadmap} that is both relevant to the career objective and logically executable for the learner.
